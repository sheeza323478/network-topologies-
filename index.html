Introduction

What is a
Network Topology?

A network topology defines the physical or logical arrangement of devices (nodes) and the connections between them in a computer network.

  • 🔗 Determines how data travels through the network
  • ⚡ Affects performance, reliability & cost
  • 🛠️ Influences fault tolerance & scalability
  • 📐 Guides physical cabling and layout

Bus Topology

Bus Topology

All devices share a single communication line called the "bus." Data sent by any device travels in both directions along the bus and is received by all connected devices.

Cable: Coaxial / Twisted Pair
Protocol: CSMA/CD (Ethernet)
Era: Early LANs (1980s–90s)

Hover over devices to interact · Signal pulses along the shared bus

Bus Topology · Advantages & Disadvantages

Bus Topology — Pros & Cons

✅ Advantages

  • Low Cost — Less cable needed than other topologies; minimal hardware required
  • Easy Installation — Simple to set up for small networks; straightforward cabling
  • Works Well for Small Networks — Sufficient for small offices or labs with few devices
  • Easy to Extend — New devices can be added by attaching to the bus

❌ Disadvantages

  • Single Point of Failure — If the main cable breaks, the entire network goes down
  • Performance Degrades — Heavy traffic causes collisions; speed drops with more devices
  • Difficult Troubleshooting — Hard to isolate faults; entire cable must be checked
  • Limited Scalability — Not suitable for large or growing networks
Bus Topology · Real-Life Examples

Bus Topology in Real Life

10Base2 Ethernet (Thinnet)

Early office LANs (1980s–90s) used coaxial cable as a shared bus. All computers connected to a single cable using BNC T-connectors — a classic Bus topology implementation.

🏢 Early Office LAN

CAN Bus in Vehicles

Modern cars use a Controller Area Network (CAN Bus) where engine control units (ECUs), ABS, airbags, and sensors all share one communication line — exactly like Bus topology.

🚗 Automotive Systems

Industrial PLC Networks

Factory automation systems use Modbus or Profibus over a shared bus cable. PLCs, sensors, and actuators communicate over a single line — Bus topology at industrial scale.

🏭 Industrial Automation
Star Topology

Star Topology

All devices connect to a central hub or switch. Communication between devices goes through the central node — no direct device-to-device connection.

Cable: Twisted Pair (UTP/STP)
Central Node: Hub / Switch / Router
Standard: IEEE 802.3 (Ethernet)

Hover over devices or hub to interact

Star Topology · Advantages & Disadvantages

Star Topology — Pros & Cons

✅ Advantages

  • Fault Tolerant — One device failure doesn't affect others; easy to isolate problems
  • Easy to Manage — Central hub makes monitoring, adding & removing devices simple
  • High Performance — Dedicated lines per device; no collisions (with a switch)
  • Scalable — Easy to grow the network by adding more devices to the switch

❌ Disadvantages

  • Central Point of Failure — If the hub/switch fails, the entire network goes down
  • Higher Cost — Requires more cable (each device needs its own wire to center)
  • Hub Dependency — Network performance limited by central device capacity
  • More Complex Setup — More cabling required than Bus topology
Star Topology · Real-Life Examples

Star Topology in Real Life

Home Wi-Fi Network

Your home router acts as the central hub. All devices (phones, laptops, smart TVs) connect to it wirelessly — a wireless Star topology used in every modern household.

🏠 Home Network

Office LAN with Central Switch

Corporate offices wire every computer and printer back to a central switch in the server room. This is the most common LAN configuration worldwide — classic Star topology.

🏢 Enterprise LAN

Cellular Base Station Networks

Mobile towers act as central hubs; your phone connects to the nearest tower. The tower connects back to a central base station controller — a Star topology at telecom scale.

📡 Telecommunications
Comparison & Conclusion

Bus vs Star — Side-by-Side

Attribute 🚌 Bus Topology ⭐ Star Topology
Cost ✅ Low (less cable) ⚠️ Higher (more cable + switch)
Fault Tolerance ❌ Poor (cable break = all down) ✅ Good (one device failure isolated)
Scalability ❌ Low (limited devices) ✅ High (easy to expand)
Performance ⚠️ Degrades with traffic ✅ Consistent (dedicated lines)
Troubleshooting ❌ Difficult (shared cable) ✅ Easy (isolate by device)
Modern Usage ⚠️ Legacy / Industrial (CAN bus) ✅ Dominant standard today

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Bus topology is cheap and simple but fragile — best for legacy or embedded systems
  • Star topology is reliable, scalable & manageable — the standard for modern networks
  • Choosing the right topology depends on cost, scale, and reliability needs
